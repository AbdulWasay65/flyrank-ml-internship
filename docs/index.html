1. Title + Abstract

Question. Can page-level Google Search Console performance signals from March 2026 help prioritize pages that subsequently improve by at least two average-position places in April?

Data. The study uses FlyRank's anonymized warehouse performance data, aggregating March decision-time signals and linking them to April outcomes at the client/content level.

Method. A Random Forest uses March impressions, clicks, and average position, with an 80/20 client-grouped split and a transparent Week-4 baseline.

Result. The Random Forest achieved 0.3685 Average Precision versus 0.2086 for the baseline, an observed gain of 0.1599 AP points; grouped ROC AUC was 0.7947.

Use. The model is best treated as a prioritization signal for human SEO/content review, not as proof of causation or an automatic publishing rule.

2. Introduction / Problem

Content teams often have more pages to review than they can realistically inspect in one pass. A useful model therefore does not need to automate editing; it needs to help answer a narrower operational question: which pages should a human look at first?

This capstone tests whether simple, decision-time search-performance signals can rank pages by their likelihood of subsequently showing a meaningful improvement in average Google Search position.

Decision boundary: the model supports review prioritization. It does not claim to predict Google's algorithm, guarantee ranking movement, or prove that an intervention caused improvement.

3. Data

The analysis uses the FlyRank internship warehouse release, including the daily fact_content_daily_performance table. The capstone aggregates observations by pseudonymized client and content page.

Decision window

March 2026 supplies the features available when the prioritization decision is made: impressions, clicks, and average position. April 2026 supplies the subsequent outcome.

Inclusion and exclusion

Pages are included when usable Google Search Console data exists for both periods and average position is non-null in both. Pages without measurable April outcomes are excluded because their improvement cannot be evaluated. Public-facing material omits client-identifying information, private URLs, queries, and other sensitive details.

4. Methodology

Features

Three March features are used: impressions, clicks, and average position. They are restricted to information available before the April outcome period.

Label

A page is positive when April average position ≤ March average position − 2. Because lower search-position values are better, this represents an improvement of at least two positions.

Model and baseline

The model is a Random Forest classifier with 150 trees, maximum depth 6, minimum leaf size 50, balanced class weights, and a fixed random seed of 42. It is compared with the Week-4 baseline using Average Precision.

Validation and leakage

The split is grouped by client_hash_id, using 80% of client groups for training and 20% for testing. This reduces the risk of placing pages from the same client in both sets. April outcome data is used only to construct the target, not as a feature. The validation audit reported grouped ROC AUC of 0.7947.

Error analysis

The model produced 8,506 predicted positives against 2,469 actual positives, including 6,469 false positives and 432 false negatives. That gap is important: a strong ranking signal can still generate many pages that require human inspection.

6. Limitations & Honest Framing

  • Association, not causation. The model identifies historical predictive signal; it does not show that changing a page feature causes a ranking improvement.
  • False positives matter. The model is intended to rank review priorities, not make binary publishing decisions.
  • Time window. The result is based on March-to-April 2026 behavior and should not be assumed to generalize unchanged to other periods.
  • Feature scope. Only three simple GSC signals were used. Content quality, search intent, technical SEO, competition, and other contextual factors are not directly modeled.
  • Data availability. Pages without reliable outcomes were excluded, so the analyzed population is not identical to every page in the warehouse.
  • Score ties. Some pages can receive identical model scores when their input features are identical, so the score alone cannot provide a complete ordering within tied groups.

The appropriate claim is therefore: the analyzed March search-performance signals contained useful directional information for prioritizing pages by subsequent improvement likelihood.

7. Ranked Recommendations

The model becomes useful when its score is paired with a disciplined human-review process.

01
Review high-scoring pages first.
Use the model score to allocate scarce editorial/SEO review time, not to auto-publish changes.
02
Validate search intent and page relevance.
Before changing content, confirm that the page matches the query intent and serves a useful purpose.
03
Inspect content quality and freshness.
Check outdated information, missing specificity, weak titles/H1s, internal linking, and useful evidence.
04
Check technical and SERP context.
Review indexing, technical issues, competing results, and other context before recommending an intervention.
05
Record the human decision.
Use APPROVE, MODIFY, REJECT, or HOLD and record the reason so the playbook can be audited.
No-go rule: do not automatically rewrite, delete, publish, or make an irreversible SEO change based only on this score. Do not treat a low score as proof that content is bad.
